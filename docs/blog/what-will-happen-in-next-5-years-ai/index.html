What Will Happen in the Next 5 Years? AI Predictions for 2031

Nine grounded predictions for 2031: agents at work, code written by software, on-device models, outcome-based pricing and a split regulatory map.

Predictions about AI are a genre of entertainment: confident, unaccountable and usually forgotten within two years. This piece tries to do something more useful. Each prediction below is tied to a trajectory you can already observe in 2026 — shipping products, announced regulation, published cost curves — and each ends with what it means for you.

None of this is a prophecy. It is a planning document. If the world turns out differently, the actions it suggests still leave you in a better position than waiting for certainty.

Agents stop chatting and start working

Between 2023 and 2026 the dominant interface for AI was a chat window you visited and prompted. That is already shifting. Modern tools chain steps together: read the email, check the spreadsheet, draft the reply, wait for approval, send. The change between now and 2031 is not intelligence — it is reliability. Agents that complete 80% of a workflow today will complete 97% of it in five years, and 97% is where background work becomes ordinary.

What it means for you: the valuable skill stops being prompting and becomes supervision — defining the task so precisely that checking the result takes a minute, and knowing which failures are expensive enough to warrant a human in the loop.

Code gets written by software, reviewed by people

"Vibe coding" went from novelty to normal in eighteen months. By 2026, generating a working prototype from a plain-English description is table stakes for any serious tool. The trajectory points at a specific 2031: first-draft code is produced by machines at near-zero marginal cost, and the scarce skill is telling the machine what to build and judging whether what it built will survive contact with production.

The bottleneck moves from typing to specification and review — which is, inconveniently, the part junior engineers used to learn by doing. Teams that keep shipping will be the ones that deliberately preserve apprenticeship paths: humans reviewing real code, not just accepting it.

What it means for you: if you write software, spend less time on boilerplate and more time on architecture, testing and the reading of unfamiliar codebases. If you hire, test for verification ability, not typing speed.

Search becomes an answer layer

Google's AI answers already resolve a large share of informational queries without a click, and every major search product is moving the same way. By 2031, searching for most facts, comparisons and how-tos will produce a synthesised answer with a handful of citations, not ten blue links. The traffic that remains flows to two places: sources good enough to be cited, and brands people type directly.

What it means for you: if you publish, being citable beats being clever — original data, clear definitions and first-hand experience are what answer engines quote. If you market, start treating direct brand search and email lists as the asset, and search traffic as the bonus.

Smaller models move onto your devices

Distillation works: today's frontier capability is routinely reproducible in models an order of magnitude smaller. Phone and laptop neural hardware is improving faster than model size. The two curves cross in the obvious place — by 2031, a genuinely capable model runs locally on your phone for daily tasks, offline, with nothing leaving the device.

What it means for you: expect a clean market split. Frontier, expensive, cloud-based models for hard reasoning; local models for private, latency-sensitive, high-volume work. Anything you would not paste into a public chat box — medical, legal, client material — becomes safe to process locally.

Pricing shifts from seats to outcomes

Per-seat pricing assumes a human drives every unit of work. Agents break that assumption: one operator supervising ten agents does not need ten licences, and vendors know it. Expect five years of messy repricing — usage credits, capacity tiers, and experimental "per outcome" contracts where you pay for a resolved ticket or a merged pull request rather than access.

What it means for you: re-check your tool stack annually against the new unit. The subscription that was obviously worth it at one human per seat may be overpriced when one person drives ten times the output — or underpriced when the vendor figures that out first.

The regulatory map splits

The EU AI Act is being phased in through the rest of the decade, US states are legislating one law at a time, and other jurisdictions are choosing between the two models or declining to act. Five years from now there is no global rule — there are tiers. Consumer products ship a compliant-by-default configuration for Europe, a looser one for the US, and geography-dependent features everywhere else.

What it means for you: compliance becomes a feature you can check for. If you handle European users or enterprise clients, prefer vendors who publish how they meet EU requirements — by 2031 that document is the difference between a five-minute procurement answer and a dead end.

Jobs change shape, not count

The honest read of the evidence so far: automation removes tasks far more reliably than it removes roles. Entry-level drafting, support triage and first-pass analysis are absorbed; judgement, accountability and taste are not. New roles appear around the machinery — evaluation, agent operations, verification — and most job titles of 2031 do not exist yet, in the same way "prompt engineer" barely existed in 2020.

What it means for you: own something checkable. A domain you can judge (a market, a codebase, a clinical area, a language pair) plus the habit of verifying machine output is a durable combination — you become the person who makes AI safe to use rather than the person it competes with.

What to do about it now

You do not need a five-year plan. You need five years of small, compounding moves:

  • Practice specification. Write down what good output looks like for your work, in detail, and refine it. That document is leverage for the whole decade.
  • Keep one skill deliberately human. Pick the part of your craft you will never delegate — interviewing, system design, editing, negotiation — and stay visibly good at it.
  • Automate one recurring task this year. Real automation experience teaches you the failure modes faster than any prediction, including these.
  • Track costs, not demos. What matters is the price per completed task, falling year over year. That curve decides what becomes ordinary.
  • Stay liquid. The people who did best through previous platform shifts changed tools quickly and kept relationships outside one company or channel.

The next five years will not be a single arrival — no switch flips, no magic morning. They will be five years of quiet substitutions: this task done by a model, that workflow supervised instead of executed, this price cut in half. The people who do well are not the ones who predicted it perfectly. They are the ones who noticed early and adapted in small steps, which is exactly what you can start doing today.

Frequently asked questions

Will AI take all jobs by 2031?

No. AI removes tasks more reliably than it removes roles. Jobs built almost entirely from routine drafting, triage and first-draft analysis will change shape, while roles that combine domain judgement with the ability to verify AI output grow. The historical pattern with software automation is task replacement before role replacement.

Will we have AGI by 2031?

Nobody can answer that honestly, because AGI has no agreed definition. What is measurable is narrower: models will keep improving on structured reasoning, coding and long-horizon tasks, and the practical question for most people is not whether a threshold is crossed, but whether their specific workflow is automated.

Which skills will matter most in the next five years?

Verification and specification. Being able to state exactly what good output looks like, and to check whether a result meets that bar, is what separates people who are made more productive by AI from people who are replaced by it. Domain depth makes the checking possible.

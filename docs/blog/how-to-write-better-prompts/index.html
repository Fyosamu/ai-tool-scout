How to Write Better Prompts: 5-Part Framework

Stop guessing at prompts. A repeatable five-part framework for getting consistent, useful output from ChatGPT, Claude and any AI assistant.

There are thousands of "prompt engineering" posts online, most of them lists of magic phrases — "act as a world-class expert", "think step by step", "unlock your full potential". Some of those help a little. None of them are a system.

What follows is a five-slot framework. Fill them, and the output becomes predictable; skip them, and you get whatever the model felt like producing.

The five-slot framework

1. Role

Who is answering? Not "an expert" — a specific role with a specific audience in mind.

Weak:  Act as a marketing expert.
Strong: You are a B2B SaaS content strategist writing for founders
        who have never hired a marketer.

The second version does real work: it tells the model what knowledge to prioritise and what vocabulary its reader will understand.

2. Task

One sentence, one outcome. If your task contains "and", split it into two prompts.

Weak:  Write something about our pricing page.
Strong: Rewrite this pricing page copy so the annual plan is
        clearly the better value.

3. Context

This is the slot most people skip, and it is the one that matters most. The model cannot see your customer, your product, or what you already tried. Give it the background you would give a new hire on their first day.

  • Who the output is for
  • What they already know
  • What has been tried and failed
  • Any source material, pasted in

4. Example

One good example beats three paragraphs of description. Show the shape you want and the model will match it far more reliably than it will follow abstract instructions.

Why examples work

Models are pattern matchers. An example gives them a concrete pattern; instructions only give them a rule to interpret. When the two conflict, the example usually wins — so make sure your example is what you actually want.

5. Constraints

Length, tone, format, and explicitly what to avoid. Negative instructions matter: "no stock phrases", "no intro paragraph", "do not summarise at the end".

The framework in one prompt

Role: You are a technical editor for a developer blog.
Task: Edit the draft below for clarity without changing its meaning.
Context: Readers are junior developers; the author tends to bury
         the main point in the third paragraph.
Example: Before: "In order to potentially improve X, one might
         consider Y." → After: "Try Y to improve X."
Constraints: Keep it under 700 words. No marketing tone.
             Preserve all code blocks exactly as written.

Three habits that improve everything

Iterate instead of rewriting

Your second prompt should reference the first: "make paragraph two shorter" is faster and more accurate than starting over with a longer prompt.

Ask the model to critique its own output

"What is weakest about this answer, and what did you leave out?" surfaces problems you would otherwise miss — and it costs one extra exchange.

Save what works

Keep a plain text file of prompts that produced results. Within a month you will have a personal library that beats anything you can download.

What no longer works

Two pieces of older advice have aged badly:

  • Chain-of-thought stuffing. Adding "think step by step" to every prompt no longer helps on modern models and can make simple answers longer than they need to be.
  • Credibility inflation. "You are the world's foremost expert" produces no better output than a specific, realistic role. Specificity beats status.

Worked example: before and after

The framework is abstract until you see the difference it makes. Here is the same task, in both forms.

Before:

Write a blog post about email marketing. Make it good
and SEO friendly. Around 1000 words.

Result: a generic 1,000-word article covering the same seven points every other article covers, with an introduction beginning "In today's fast-paced digital landscape". It needed a full rewrite to be publishable.

After — same task, five slots filled:

Role: You are an email strategist writing for B2B SaaS
      founders with lists under 2,000 subscribers.
Task: Write one article on improving re-engagement
      campaigns for dormant subscribers.
Context: Our readers send weekly newsletters and get
         38% open rates but under 2% clicks. They have
         tried a discount offer already — it failed.
Example: Hook style — "Your quietest subscribers aren't
         uninterested. They're unconvinced."
Constraints: 1,000 words. No generic "build your list"
             advice. Include one concrete segment table.
             End with a single recommended next action.

Result: a draft that needs light editing rather than a rewrite. The difference is not a clever phrase — it is that the second prompt removes every decision the model would otherwise have guessed at.

Common mistakes

  • Asking for everything at once. A prompt covering five deliverables produces five shallow ones. One prompt, one outcome.
  • Hiding the real constraint. If the limit is "must fit in a 200-character field", say so. Models cannot infer a constraint you did not state.
  • Never showing what good looks like. Without an example, "make it better" is unactionable — better than what, by what measure?
  • Skipping context you assume is obvious. You know your product, your reader and your history with the topic. The model knows none of it.
One prompt, one job

If you find yourself writing "and also", stop. Split it into a second prompt that references the first. Chained prompts give you an intermediate result you can correct, rather than a single output you can only accept or reject.

Quick checklist

Before you hit send

Five seconds, five questions.

  • Did I say who the audience is?
  • Is there exactly one task in this prompt?
  • Did I include context the model cannot guess?
  • Is there an example of the output I want?
  • Did I state length, tone and what to avoid?

None of this is secret knowledge. The difference between a useful answer and a generic one is usually those five lines you did not write.

Frequently asked questions

What is the simplest way to get better AI output?

State four things in every prompt: the role the AI should take, the exact task, a concrete example of the desired output, and the constraints such as length, tone and what to avoid.

Does prompt engineering still matter in 2026?

Yes, but less than before. Modern models infer intent better, so clever phrasing matters less. Being specific about context, audience and format still makes a large difference.

How long should a prompt be?

As long as the context requires and no longer. A two-line prompt with a clear example usually beats a 300-word prompt of instructions nobody would follow.
